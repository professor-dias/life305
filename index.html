The Problem

Preventable Premature Mortality Among Transgender Women

Between 2017 and 2023, 263 transgender people lost their lives to violent hate crimes in the United States. Around 193 of those deaths involved a firearm (McLellan et al., 2026). Over 60% of those victims were Black transgender women. Additionally, Black and Latina transgender women account for more than 80% of HIV cases in this population nationally (CDC, 2024).

Substance use is another risk factor leading to overdose and early mortality. Barriers are often multi-level, fueled by discrimination, social rejection, lack of opportunities, homelessness, and poor mental health, leading to death by suicide or chronic disease / poor quality-of-life.

Ultimately, transgender women are losing their lives early, and disproportionately. Five causes drive that mortality. All five are preventable.

Transgender women report high rates of anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation. Chronic minority stress, anti-trans stigma, and limited social support compound psychiatric burden. Transphobia and social rejection are direct drivers of suicidal ideation in this population.

Sources: Wanta et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2015

Substance use, particularly methamphetamine and crack/cocaine, is strongly linked to HIV acquisition and condomless anal sex among transgender women. Risk increases when substance use intersects with survival sex work.

Sources: Hsiang et al., 2022; Cyrus et al., 2023

Most transgender homicides are hate-motivated. Many go unrecorded: law enforcement agencies frequently misclassify victims when gender identity is not documented at the scene, meaning official counts understate the true toll. Violence is concentrated in the South.

Sources: McLellan et al., 2026; Everytown Research & Policy, 2024

Transgender women are among the groups at highest risk for HIV, yet they remain among the least likely to access prevention and treatment. Medical mistrust, transphobia in clinical settings, and structural barriers keep many from seeking care even when it is available. HIV does not cause early death on its own. Delayed diagnosis and interrupted treatment do.

Sources: Maragh-Bass et al., 2024; Holder et al., 2019; CDC, 2024

Chronic stress, limited social support, financial hardship, and barriers to gender-affirming care compound poor physical health outcomes. Discrimination limits access to employment, housing, and healthcare, driving a cycle of structural vulnerability and preventable illness.

Sources: Fredriksen Goldsen et al., 2022; Hobfoll, 1989

Why South Florida?

Addressing Disparity, and Actioning Change

Transgender lives are at the center of a national debate, especially in Florida right now, over: gender-affirming care for children, bathrooms, sports, and a lot more (Florida SB 254, 2023; Florida HB 1475, 2021; ACLU, 2024; Movement Advancement Project, 2024).

This study does not engage in any of that. It focuses on what happens after the debate.

Adult transgender women, 18 and older, most of who are American citizens and residents, are living with the consequences. Many are sleeping outside tonight in Miami-Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach. They cannot afford an apartment. Many do not know where the next meal will coming from (Marcus, 2024).

These transwomen did not start this fight. But, they are living inside it.

South Florida’s tri-county region: Miami-Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach, carries one of the highest HIV prevalence rates in the country and a large transgender population with substantial barriers to affirming care (Holder et al., 2019; Cyrus et al., 2021; Maragh-Bass et al., 2024). Little research has examined how transgender women in this region use digital health tools (Bonato et al., 2025; Shboul et al., 2023; Raile, 2024). No AI-driven, culturally tailored digital health tool has been developed and tested to improve access to care for transgender women in South Florida.

Study Aims

AIM 1

Analyze existing quantitative and qualitative data from the TRUST study and the SHARC Florida Cohort Study Wave 3 to identify predictors associated with preventable premature mortality among transgender women in Florida and develop a precision-prevention intervention framework.

AIM 2

Conduct one-on-one qualitative in-depth interviews with transgender women (N=14) and a Community Advisory Board (N=6) to refine the intervention framework and co-design content for the AI-driven precision-prevention mobile application.

AIM 3

Develop the AI-driven precision-prevention mobile application and pretest its feasibility, usability, and acceptability among transgender women (N = 12).

The LIFE 305 App

What is LIFE 305?

LIFE 305 is an AI-driven, precision-prevention mobile application built specifically for transgender women in South Florida. It combines a personalized risk-profiling screener, AI chatbot coaching, micro-learning modules, and geo-linked referrals to connect each woman to the resources most relevant to her. Every interaction is culturally tailored, trauma-informed, and affirming.

Built on three frameworks

1

Conservation of Resources (COR) Theory

Frames health burden as the loss of social, psychological, and material resources under chronic stress; guides profiling of risk and need.

2

Social Cognitive Theory

Explains behavior change through personal, behavioral, and environmental factors; guides skill-building, self-efficacy coaching, and access.

3

UTAUT

Guides the design and evaluation of the AI-driven components, to ensure participants adopt and keep using the app.
